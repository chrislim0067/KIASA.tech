Google Is Building an Off Switch for AI Overviews. Read the Small Print Before You Reach for It.

← Back to Blog

Google has started testing something website owners have been asking for since AI Overviews appeared: a switch that takes your site out of them.

It is a real control, it is documented, and the first instinct of almost every business owner who hears about it is the wrong one. Here is what it actually does.

What Google announced

In its own announcement, Google describes a toggle inside Search Console that lets a site owner decide whether their site appears in — and helps ground responses in — generative AI features on Search. That covers AI Overviews, AI Mode, and AI Overviews in Discover.

Two sentences from that post matter more than the rest:

Sites that opt out will not receive traffic or impressions from these features. … This control will not be used as a ranking signal for search results outside of these generative AI Search features.

Read those together. The switch is narrow and it is honest about being narrow: flipping it costs you the AI surfaces and, by Google’s account, leaves your ordinary rankings alone.

It is being tested with a subset of website owners in the UK first, with wider availability later. Search Engine Land reports that Google began taking the control into account on 17 June 2026 — that date is theirs, not Google’s. If you are reading this from Singapore, the honest answer is: check your own Search Console, because we cannot confirm it has reached the GCC.

The thing the switch does not do

This is where the instinct goes wrong.

Opting out does not remove AI Overviews. It does not reduce how often they appear, change how people search, or claw back the click that an AI answer absorbed. It removes you from a surface that carries on without you — and something else fills the space.

Search Engine Land put it about as bluntly as it can be put: if a large travel brand opted out tomorrow, people would not stop planning holidays. Google would simply surface someone else.

So the decision is not “do I approve of AI Overviews”. It is: would I rather be quoted in the answer my customer reads, or absent from it? For most businesses selling a service in a competitive market, that question answers itself.

The report that shipped alongside it is the actual news

Buried under the more dramatic headline is the thing that changes what you can do on Monday morning. Google has introduced Search Generative AI performance reports in Search Console, announced on 3 June 2026.

What it gives you:

  • Impressions — how often URLs from your site appeared in generative AI features on Search and Discover
  • Pages — which of your URLs appeared
  • Countries — where they appeared
  • Devices, and the usual date granularity

What it does not give you, and this is the part to internalise:

  • No click data. Google is not reporting how many people clicked through from an AI response to your site. Asked about it, a Google spokesperson told Search Engine Land the company is still working out which insights help most and will add metrics over time.
  • No query data. You cannot see which questions surfaced you.
  • No position data. There is no ranking to track.

An impressions-only report is still a step change from nothing, and it is free and first-party. But be clear-eyed about what it can prove. It can tell you whether you are being surfaced at all and roughly how that trends. It cannot tell you what that visibility was worth.

Anyone selling you an “AI visibility” retainer should be measured against it

We have written before that there is no such thing as AI SEO in Google’s own documentation — the optimisations that apply are the ones that always applied. This report is the practical follow-on: for the first time there is a free, first-party number to hold that kind of proposal against.

If someone is quoting you a monthly fee for AI visibility work, ask them three questions:

  1. What is our baseline impression count in the Search Generative AI report today? If they have not looked, they are selling before diagnosing.
  2. What will you report in ninety days, and from which source? A screenshot of a third-party “AI visibility score” is a vendor’s estimate. Search Console is Google’s own count.
  3. What are you claiming about ChatGPT, Perplexity or Claude? None of them publishes a citation report. Any number quoted for those is an estimate, however confidently it is presented.

Who might legitimately opt out

There is a real case for it, and it is narrower than the discussion suggests.

It can make sense if the article itself is the product. A publisher whose revenue comes from people reading the page — ad impressions, subscriptions, licensed archives — is in a genuine bind when the answer is delivered without the visit. For them, opting out is a commercial calculation with a number on both sides.

It rarely makes sense if the page is a route to something else. A clinic, a law firm, a contractor, a studio, a hotel, a store. Your page is not the product; it is how someone finds the product. Being cited in the answer a buyer reads is closer to a shop window than to giving away inventory.

There is also a middle position worth knowing about, because it is not the same decision: Google documents page-level and snippet-level controls that have existed for years. Those are a scalpel; the Search Console toggle is a main breaker. If your concern is one specific document rather than your whole site, look at the scalpel first.

What we would actually do this month

Not much, and deliberately so — because the switch is not the urgent part.

1. Open Search Console and find your baseline. If the Search Generative AI report is available on your property, record this month’s impressions and which pages are appearing. If it is not available yet, note that too. A baseline you did not take is a comparison you cannot make later.

2. Leave the toggle alone. It is in limited testing, it is one-directional in effect, and there is no deadline forcing a decision. Nothing is gained by being early to a switch that only subtracts.

3. Check what your own analytics already tell you. Most businesses have more first-party data than they use — where enquiries come from, which pages precede them, which channel actually produces work rather than traffic. That is the measurement that survives any change to how search results are drawn.

4. Separate the two questions. “Am I visible in AI answers?” and “Is my website producing business?” are different questions with different data behind them. Conflating them is how agencies sell activity as outcomes.

The short version

Google built an off switch, told you plainly that it costs you traffic and impressions from AI features, and told you just as plainly that it will not be held against you elsewhere. That is a more transparent deal than the industry usually gets.

But an off switch is not a strategy, and for most businesses reaching for it would be paying a real cost to make a point. The report that arrived with it is the useful half — imperfect, impressions-only, and still the first honest number anyone has been given on this.

Take the baseline. Leave the switch.
