AI Just Split Into Free and Frontier. Here Is What That Means for Singapore Businesses.

← Back to Blog

This month OpenAI made its cheapest model unlimited for free users, and kept its best model behind the paywall. Most coverage read that as a giveaway. It is closer to a line being drawn through a market — and the same line is about to be drawn through yours.

What actually happened

In the week of 6 August, GPT-5.6 Luna became the default model for Free and Go accounts, with unlimited text conversations following shortly after, alongside a Think button for harder questions. Paid Plus and Pro accounts received an upgraded GPT-5.6 Sol: more reliable with facts, more focused in its answers, with a slider controlling how much thought it puts into a response.

The detail that most summaries skipped is the one that matters commercially. Luna is OpenAI's cheapest GPT-5.6 tier, not its flagship. Free users did not receive the frontier model; they received the economical one, without limits. The Think button lets Luna work harder on a difficult prompt. It does not open the door to Sol.

OpenAI published the gap itself. In its own testing, responses containing factual errors were 68% less common with Sol than with the previous GPT-5.5 Instant, and 62% less common with Luna. The company measured the difference between its good tier and its better tier, disclosed it, and put a price on the better one.

Free did not mean equal

That is a more instructive event than a giveaway would have been. The most aggressive AI company in the world had the option to make its best work free, and instead concluded that a meaningful share of the market will pay for the superior version when the difference is real and legible.

If you sell on quality rather than price, that is your argument, made by someone else, at scale.

The strategic consequence is not that AI became free. It is that capable AI became free and unlimited, while excellent AI stayed paid — and customers are now fluent in the difference. They use the free tier all day. They know exactly what "good enough, instantly, at no cost" feels like. That is the new floor, and it is not a flattering one to be measured against.

Two resets, not one

The first is the experience floor. Every leap in consumer technology quietly rewrites what "good" feels like. When smartphones became universal, sites that were not mobile-first began to feel broken. When same-day delivery became normal, "ships in 5–7 days" became a reason to abandon a cart.

Your customers now spend hours each week with a system that answers instantly, understands context, and never makes them repeat themselves. Then they arrive at a business website and are asked to complete a contact form and wait two working days. That contrast will not appear in your analytics as a single event. It appears as slowly rising bounce rates and quietly falling enquiry quality.

The second reset is quieter and further-reaching. As unlimited AI conversation becomes the default way people research — "best interior fit-out company in Singapore", "is this brand credible", "compare these two clinics" — visibility stops being only a question of ranking on a results page. It becomes a question of whether AI systems can find your business, understand what you do, and cite you with confidence.

This is answer engine optimisation, and it rewards a specific kind of website: clean structure, precise service descriptions, consistent business information, and content that answers questions rather than gesturing at them. Thin brochure sites are becoming invisible twice over — once to human browsers, and again to the systems those humans now consult first.

When the tools are common, judgement is the product

Here is the part that should be encouraging if you compete on quality. When capable AI is free for everyone, access to AI is no longer an advantage. Your competitors have the same models. So does a freelancer producing generic websites at volume.

What cannot be downloaded is judgement — the strategy, taste and restraint that decide where intelligence belongs in a customer journey and where it would cheapen it. The market is dividing into businesses that bolt a chatbot onto an average website, and businesses that design intelligence into the experience itself: an enquiry concierge that qualifies serious prospects with discretion, product journeys that adapt to the visitor, follow-ups that arrive at the right moment in the right tone.

The first group looks automated. The second looks effortless. Customers register the difference even when they cannot articulate it.

What we would do first

  1. Audit the intelligence gap. Walk your own customer journey with a ChatGPT conversation open beside it. Every moment your website feels slower, vaguer or more repetitive than the assistant your customer just left is a gap with a cost attached.
  2. Make your site legible to machines. Structured data, precise service pages, consistent naming, real answers to real questions. This is the foundation of being cited rather than merely found.
  3. Introduce intelligence with restraint. One well-designed AI touchpoint, implemented properly, outperforms five widgets bolted on. Restraint reads as confidence; clutter reads as insecurity.
  4. Protect the brand voice. Generic AI output is flooding every channel. Distinctive positioning, original photography and a recognisable tone have quietly become defensive assets.

The view from KIASA

We build digital experiences for brands that compete on quality rather than price, and this moment favours exactly those brands. Free AI raises the floor for everyone. It does nothing to raise the ceiling. The ceiling is still set by strategy, design and execution — and OpenAI has just spent a product launch demonstrating that people will pay for the better tier when the difference is real.

Make sure yours is real, and make sure it is visible.
